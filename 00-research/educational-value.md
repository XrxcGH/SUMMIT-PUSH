# educational-value

## KEY TAKEAWAYS
- Rapid React (2022) is the consensus best all-around teaching game: intake, serializer, shooter, optional turret, and highly varied climbers in one season, with tasks 'achievable for teams of all levels' (Karthik, CD thread 'What did Rapid React get RIGHT?')
- Power Up (2018) is the mentors' favorite practice/training game because its mechanisms (elevator, roller claw, rung climber) 'have been recycled a lot in recent games' — ideal for offseason CAD exercises
- Best design-for-education principle found (s-neff, Crescendo vs Reefscape thread): the program inspires most when perceived and actual difficulty align — Crescendo's 'looks hard is easy' scoring inspired students; Reefscape's 'looks easy is hard' algae/deep-climb produced 'lots of frustrated teenagers to manage'
- Low floor/high ceiling done right: Rapid React's low goal + low rungs, Crescendo's amp + 2024 KitBot (a rookie kitbot team captained an 8th alliance), Charged Up's cubes; done wrong: Reefscape's deceptive floor where 'the bottom falls out' below the top tier, and Recycle Rush's cliff for weak fields
- 2022-2025 lesson map: 2022 taught fundamentals/flywheels/climb sequencing post-COVID; 2023 taught arm-vs-elevator tradeoffs and the perils of pure optimization ('could have been run in a simulation'); 2024 taught strategy, alliance roles, and AprilTag auto-aim; 2025 taught forced design tradeoffs and made precision vision/auto-align nearly mandatory
- Crescendo is the community's pick for most strategically educational recent game (mechanically simple, 'insanely in depth game strategy'); Reefscape is the pick for most design-tradeoff-educational; opinion is genuinely split on which season was better overall
- Effective offseason design challenges (1678 Storm Surge CADathon, Pack Challenge) share: a tight 9-11 day window simulating build season, judging criteria withheld until kickoff, small team caps, deliverables of CAD + written rationale + short video, and public expert feedback (live judged Twitch show / industry-engineer design reviews)
- Scaffold before CAD: mock kickoffs with old reveal videos, physical prototyping systems (Spectrum ProtoPipe, HypeBlocks), Lego-based CAD onboarding, and copying elite robots to learn design intent are all mentor-endorsed teaching techniques
- Disengagement drivers to avoid in any teaching game: no alliance interaction (Recycle Rush, late-season Reefscape), dead or inexplicable mechanics (control panel, trap, processor), pervasive robot damage (Crescendo collisions), foul-decided matches (Stronghold), and all-or-nothing endgames (Steamworks climb)
- Deep Space's enduring mentor lesson: winners 'built something relatively simple and executed well' — feature count loses to execution, a core message for student design reviews
- Institutional teaching models worth copying: 1678 Fall Workshops + seven-track training resources, Spectrum 3847 elective camps + rookie Everybot/second-robot builds, JVN's transparent daily build journals, and Karthik's Effective FIRST Strategies for strategy education

## REPORT
# Which FRC Games Teach Best? A Research Synthesis from Mentor Discussions, Team Blogs, and Community Retrospectives

This report synthesizes mentor and educator opinion from Chief Delphi retrospective threads (fetched in full), team-published training resources (1678 Citrus Circuits, Spectrum 3847, 1114 Simbotics, 148/JVN), and FIRST-adjacent articles, focused on which games teach the most and why, and what makes offseason design challenges effective teaching tools.

## 1. Games That Forced the Widest Variety of Mechanisms

**2022 Rapid React** is the community's consensus "full-stack mechanism game." In ["What did Rapid React get RIGHT?"](https://www.chiefdelphi.com/t/what-did-rapid-react-get-right/410496), Karthik Kanagasabapathy (legendary 1114 strategy mentor) called it "one of the best FRC games of all time," noting "the tasks in the game led to a large variety of robot designs." A competitive Rapid React robot needed a ground intake, indexer/serializer, flywheel shooter (optionally turreted), drivetrain, and a traversal climber — user crueter noted "I saw several dozen climbs and I don't think there were two identical climbs at all," and that the game let "low-resource/skill teams build competitive robots... with more advanced teams opting for the high end stuff, like windmill/otherwise very fast climbs, turrets." In the ["Best game for practice CAD"](https://www.chiefdelphi.com/t/best-game-for-practice-cad/434157) thread, student designer Takanori recommended it specifically for training: "the packaging around the climber, shooter, intakes, transfer/magazine, and electronics really made me think. I'd recommend Rapid React 100%."

**2020/2021 Infinite Recharge** demanded the longest single-robot subsystem list of the modern era (intake, indexer, turreted shooter, vision targeting, buddy-less climber, control-panel spinner), but mentors consider it *too much*: in the Rapid React thread, theIme wrote "Games like Infinite Recharge were too complicated... I still don't really know the point of the color wheel, and I was a FRC member for that season."

**2025 Reefscape** is the most recent "forced tradeoffs" game. In the [Reefscape complexity poll](https://www.chiefdelphi.com/t/poll-reefscape-as-the-most-complex-game-in-recent-memory/483702), Ryan_Swanson wrote: "This game is definitely fun from a robot design perspective. There are forced tradeoffs with every strategic decision. Even if your team can do everything you'll still be required to make performance limiting sacrifices." Eliot noted almost no robot could do everything (L1–L4 coral + net algae + ground intake both pieces + deep climb) under the weight limit. Reefscape robots combined elevators, articulated end effectors, ground intakes, algae mechanisms, and deep-climb winches — arguably the widest manipulator variety of 2022–2025.

**2018 Power Up** is repeatedly cited as the best *training* game because its mechanisms recycle: in the CAD-practice thread, mentor pevvvvvvvvv: "I used to use 2018 as a practice exercise for my newbies! The sort of mechanisms it uses (elevator, roller claw, rung climber) have been recycled a lot in recent games."

**2023 Charged Up** produced surprising design diversity for a pick-and-place game. In [The Charged Up Retrospective](https://www.chiefdelphi.com/t/the-charged-up-retrospective/443693), e172: "There were a surprising amount of ways to pick-and-place game elements... This led to more variability among robots, and not settling on a clear-cut top concept like [1323's] cube intake in 2018."

## 2. "Low Floor, High Ceiling": Examples and Counter-Examples

FIRST explicitly designs for this: a 2013 [Game Design Committee Q&A thread](https://www.chiefdelphi.com/t/faf-june-28-2013-game-design-committee/130154) confirms the GDC "attempts to create games that have a low floor but a high ceiling... tasks that very basic robots can support, along with tasks that require more sophisticated designs."

**Best examples:**
- **2022 Rapid React** — low goal + low climb rungs were meaningful for rookies; traversal climbs and turrets for elites. Karthik: "All tasks looked hard to the audience, but were very achievable for teams of all levels, while still providing appropriate engineering challenges." gavandavis292: "You just need some drive practice, a half decent intake, an accurate shooter, and some sort of climb to be a little bit competitive."
- **2024 Crescendo** — the amp made the "easy" task strategically essential (kindagood in [Crescendo vs Reefscape](https://www.chiefdelphi.com/t/crescendo-vs-reefscape/503365)), and the 2024 KitBot (per the [FRC Blog KitBot threads](https://www.chiefdelphi.com/t/frc-blog-looking-back-on-the-2024-kitbot-moving-forward/469042)) let rookie teams shoot into the speaker on day one — one rookie kitbot team became an 8th-seed alliance captain, and pushbot counts dropped visibly. This is the strongest recent "floor-raising" intervention.
- **2023 Charged Up** — Leap called it "the game Recycle Rush should have been. Everyone had a way to participate in a meaningful way which was great for teams all across the spectrum." Pizzatc: cubes made it "possible for lower level teams to still put up high scores."

**Counter-examples / cautionary tales:**
- **2025 Reefscape** had a nominal floor (L1 trough, processor, Everybot archetype) but mentors found the floor deceptive. s-neff in [Favorite FRC Game (2025 Updated)](https://www.chiefdelphi.com/t/favorite-frc-game-2025-updated/504677): "Absolutely brutal robot design challenge, everything that looks easy is hard, lots of frustrated teenagers to manage." Lucs100 in [Review of Reefscape](https://www.chiefdelphi.com/t/review-of-reefscape/499842): "At any level of play below the very top the bottom falls out pretty quickly. Strong teams can cycle to L4 several times faster than weaker teams can cycle to L1... We are talking about essentially completely different games between the different 'tiers' of teams." The ceiling was superb; the floor was mislabeled.
- **2015 Recycle Rush** — Ryan_Swanson's [Recycle Rush Reflection](https://www.chiefdelphi.com/t/a-recycle-rush-reflection/143576): "the flaws become more evident at the lower levels"; at weak regionals matches were dead, while deep fields (Midwest) were exciting — i.e., the floor was a cliff.
- **2019 Deep Space** required precision placement of two game pieces under heavy defense; the [5987 Throwback thread](https://www.chiefdelphi.com/t/frc-throwback-series-destination-deep-space-2019-strategy-lessons-top-3-robots/507936) notes the winning lesson was that top teams (973, 3538) "built something relatively simple and executed well" — a good teaching case that elegance beats feature count.

**The sharpest educational framing found** comes from s-neff (mentor, in Crescendo vs Reefscape): "I loved that Crescendo had 'looks easy is easy' (single chain climbing), 'looks hard is easy' (scoring notes) and 'looks hard is hard' tasks (trap). I was frustrated that Reefscape algae scoring and deep climb was 'looks easy is hard'... **The program is most inspirational when perceived and actual difficulty are aligned, or when actual difficulty is lower than perceived difficulty.**" This is arguably the single best design-for-education principle in the entire corpus.

## 3. Design Lessons Mentors Attribute to Each Recent Game (2022–2025)

**2022 Rapid React — the "fundamentals reboot."** Coming out of COVID, mentors valued its scoped challenge: Lil_Lavery: "an appropriate-level of engineering challenge to come back from a season with no robots... Tasks we had seen before with a twist. Readily available solutions demonstrated in the very recent past." TGilmore533: "Instead of throwing them to the wolves, it gave teams a chance to regroup and get back to the basics." Lessons taught: projectile physics/flywheel tuning, ball-path serialization, and the traversal climb taught linkage/hook design and sequencing (climb state machines).

**2023 Charged Up — kinematics, superstructures, and the limits of optimization.** Taught arm vs. elevator tradeoff analysis, double-jointed arm control (many teams' first exposure to feedforward on multi-DOF arms), and cone/cube end-effector compliance. Michael_Corsetto (1678 design mentor) summarized tersely: "Many teams could score something / Endgame too easy / Diminishing returns scoring / Not much interaction with opponent... Bonus: 4ft extensions = very unsafe. Never again." Leap's critique is a strategy-teaching lesson in itself: "Regional winners were determined in Build Season... This was a game about optimization... this game could have been run in a simulation with very little difference in outcome."

**2024 Crescendo — strategy, alliance play, and vision.** Widely called the most *strategically* educational recent game. art3misfowel: "Crescendo is a mechanically simple game, with basic robot strategy, but insanely in depth game strategy." It taught on-the-fly auto-aim (first full AprilTag season put shoot-while-moving and pose estimation in mid-tier reach), passing plays, amp/speaker economy, and role-switching between matches. In [Thoughts on Crescendo a few weeks in](https://www.chiefdelphi.com/t/thoughts-on-crescendo-a-few-weeks-in/460982), benmoeller: "Being good at both [speaker and amp] adds a ton of value" versus prior years where "you can be really good at one thing and do fine." Cautionary lessons: seg9585's "The trap is a trap. Basically worthless in elims compared to its design difficulty" (cost/benefit scoping), and ChiefK33f's complaint that mid-season "meta" rebuilds made "strategic design useless" at the top.

**2025 Reefscape — tradeoff engineering and software leverage.** Darre: "The biggest positive for this game is the design challenge; so many teams had to make a sacrifice in one way or another. Even the top teams had to make design cuts until the optimal archetype revealed itself a la ground intake." Thor5090: "Reefscape definitely tested a team's ability to program vs. building a robot. Without the ability to incorporate vision with precision auto alignment teams were significantly at a disadvantage" — the strongest recent forcing function for teaching reef-relative pose control and auto-align. MrPotato noted the meta *inverted* healthily: "this year L1 and algae became more important as teams got better" (unlike Charged Up which "felt solved before Houston"). Richard_Wallace: "Iteration is the soul of innovation. This game challenged all of us to iterate right up to the end."

## 4. What Makes an Offseason Design Challenge Effective for Teaching

Concrete patterns from community-run challenges:

- **Tight, simulated-build-season scoping.** The [1678 Storm Surge CADathon](https://www.chiefdelphi.com/t/1678-storm-surge-cadathon/396100) ran 9 days across two weekends, with judging criteria withheld until kickoff ("DO NOT START... Crucial judging criteria that will fundamentally determine the design of your robot will not be released before then") — deliberately teaching requirements-driven design rather than portfolio polishing. Team-size caps (3 members across teams) prevented all-star stacking.
- **Defined deliverables beyond CAD.** Storm Surge required CAD files, renders, and a written submission form; the [Pack Challenge](https://www.chiefdelphi.com/t/pack-challenge-a-real-world-offseason-cad-project-for-frc-teams/501254) (PMMI-backed palletizer design) requires a 5-minute video "that communicates your design solution," design documentation, and optional renders — explicitly building "CAD, documentation, and presentation skills."
- **Public expert feedback as the payoff.** Storm Surge culminated in a live judged Twitch show with a 1678 mentor/student panel; Pack Challenge advertises "feedback from real industry engineers" and a professional-style design review. Participant kogappa (LigerBots 2877) posted a full design rationale afterward — evidence the format elicits reflective writeups (they deliberately *cut* the high goal after a points-per-cycle analysis, a scoping lesson in miniature).
- **Choosing the right practice game.** In the CAD-practice thread, mentors converge on: pick a mechanism-transferable game (2018, 2020, 2022), or use purpose-built CADathon games ("you can compare your designs to the 'best' designs from that CADathon" — yadin), or simply copy an elite team's robot ("Copying bots lets you... learn the intent of how things are designed" — howlongismyname). Nick.kremer's meta-advice: motivation beats curriculum — "simply attempting something will bring you a wealth of experience."
- **Hands-on scaffolding before CAD.** In [Hands On FRC Design Training](https://www.chiefdelphi.com/t/hands-on-frc-design-training/415046), Nick.kremer recommends physical prototyping systems (Spectrum's [ProtoPipe](https://spectrum3847.org/protopipe-rapid-prototyping-system/), HypeBlocks, TTB blocks) "so they can focus on what to build not how to build it," plus "mock kickoffs" using old reveal videos (LuiTheFly) and 2D paper-linkage climber exercises (sgeckler). In [Design Challenges](https://www.chiefdelphi.com/t/design-challenges/506815), a design lead reports teaching CAD via Lego re-modeling as faster than tool-by-tool lessons.
- **Institutionalized programs.** 1678's [Fall Workshops](https://www.citruscircuits.org/fallworkshops.html) and [training resources](https://www.citruscircuits.org/training-resources) (seven subteam tracks, recorded lectures incl. Corsetto's Strategic Design series); Spectrum 3847 runs elective skill camps, new-student Everybot/MCC robot builds so rookies "build, wire, and program a robot before the design for their actual robot is complete" ([Spectrum blog](http://blog.spectrum3847.org/2020/01/2020-day-14-controls-and-everybot.html)); JVN's [build journals](https://johnvneun.com/jvns-robots) model transparent daily design reasoning ("There are no failures, only lessons"); Karthik's [Effective FIRST Strategies](https://www.simbotics.org/resources/strategy) remains the canonical strategy-education deck.

## 5. Game Mechanics That Caused Student Disengagement

- **No alliance interaction / solved games.** Recycle Rush (2015) is the archetype: "There was no energy from the crowd... I heard the same message over and over: 'This game is boring'" (Ryan_Swanson). Orthofort's nuance matters for educators: the game "appeals more to the actual people involved with the robot" — great design challenge, dead competition — and retention suffers when only the design kids are fed. Charged Up drew the same critique in milder form ("could have been run in a simulation" — Leap).
- **Perceived-easy-but-actually-hard tasks.** Reefscape's algae/deep climb (s-neff: "lots of frustrated teenagers to manage") and unclosable tier gaps (Lucs100) demoralize mid-tier students.
- **Inexplicable or dead mechanics.** Infinite Recharge's control panel ("I still don't really know the point of the color wheel" — theIme), Crescendo's trap and random microphone scoring (seg9585), Reefscape's post-quals-dead processor (multiple posters). Mechanisms students build but never meaningfully use teach cynicism.
- **Pervasive robot damage.** Crescendo's open-field collisions: "We did a robot rebuild... because our robot was destroyed... that's drained my whole team to the dregs. A game where robot damage is so pervasive is a bad game" (anon, Thoughts-on-Crescendo thread).
- **Foul-decided matches and refereeing variance.** Stronghold "will be remembered as the year of fouls... the finals of Einstein were decided on a foul" ([Stronghold thread](https://www.chiefdelphi.com/t/what-will-frc-2016-stronghold-be-remembered-by/151928)); Knufire on Rapid React: "most of the gripes... are with its refereeing and not the game design."
- **All-or-nothing endgames.** Steamworks' climb ("if you missed a climb the whole effort was wasted" — nuclearnerd) and 2017's near-impossible-for-average-teams fuel shooting (Kevin_Leonard: teams "won/lost matches at random... based entirely on whether their partners climbed").

## Synthesis

For an educational offseason challenge, the community evidence points to: use Rapid React or Power Up-class mechanism sets (intake + serializer + shooter/placer + climber) for breadth with achievable physics; borrow Crescendo's strategy layer (a low-skill task that stays strategically vital) for engagement; borrow Reefscape's forced-tradeoff structure for design-thinking depth but honor s-neff's alignment principle — make perceived difficulty match actual difficulty; and package it like the 1678/Pack Challenge model: fixed short window, criteria revealed at kickoff, CAD + written rationale + short video deliverables, and a public expert-judged review show.
