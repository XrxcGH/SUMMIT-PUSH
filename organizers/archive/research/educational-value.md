# Educational Value of FRC Games

## Key takeaways
- Rapid React (2022) is the consensus best all-around teaching game. One season covered an intake, a serializer, a shooter, an optional turret and a wide variety of climbers, with tasks 'achievable for teams of all levels' (Karthik, CD thread 'What did Rapid React get RIGHT?').
- Power Up (2018) is the mentors' favorite game for practice and training because its mechanisms (elevator, roller claw, rung climber) 'have been recycled a lot in recent games'. That makes it well suited to offseason CAD exercises.
- The clearest design-for-education principle found (s-neff, Crescendo vs Reefscape thread): the program inspires most when perceived and actual difficulty align. Crescendo's 'looks hard is easy' scoring inspired students; Reefscape's 'looks easy is hard' algae and deep climb produced 'lots of frustrated teenagers to manage'.
- Low floor and high ceiling done well: Rapid React's low goal and low rungs, Crescendo's amp and the 2024 KitBot (a rookie kitbot team captained an 8th alliance), and Charged Up's cubes. Done badly: Reefscape's deceptive floor, where 'the bottom falls out' below the top tier, and Recycle Rush's cliff for weak fields.
- 2022-2025 lessons: 2022 taught fundamentals, flywheels and climb sequencing after COVID; 2023 taught arm-versus-elevator tradeoffs and the limits of pure optimization ('could have been run in a simulation'); 2024 taught strategy, alliance roles and AprilTag auto-aim; 2025 taught forced design tradeoffs and made precise vision and auto-align nearly mandatory.
- The community picks Crescendo as the most strategically educational recent game (mechanically simple, with 'insanely in depth game strategy') and Reefscape as the most educational for design tradeoffs. Opinion is split on which season was better overall.
- Effective offseason design challenges (1678 Storm Surge CADathon, Pack Challenge) have common features: a tight 9-11 day window that simulates build season, judging criteria withheld until kickoff, small team caps, deliverables of CAD plus a written rationale and a short video, and public expert feedback (a live judged Twitch show, or design reviews by industry engineers).
- Scaffolding before CAD works. Mentors endorse mock kickoffs with old reveal videos, physical prototyping systems (Spectrum ProtoPipe, HypeBlocks), Lego-based CAD onboarding, and copying elite robots to learn design intent.
- Causes of disengagement to avoid in any teaching game: no alliance interaction (Recycle Rush, late-season Reefscape), dead or inexplicable mechanics (control panel, trap, processor), frequent robot damage (Crescendo collisions), matches decided by fouls (Stronghold) and all-or-nothing endgames (Steamworks climb).
- The lasting mentor lesson from Deep Space is that the winners 'built something relatively simple and executed well'. Execution beats feature count, which is a core message for student design reviews.
- Institutional teaching models worth copying: 1678's Fall Workshops and seven-track training resources, Spectrum 3847's elective camps and rookie Everybot/second-robot builds, JVN's public daily build journals, and Karthik's Effective FIRST Strategies for strategy education.

## Sources

This report summarizes mentor and educator opinion on which games teach the most and why, and on what makes an offseason design challenge an effective teaching tool. The sources are Chief Delphi retrospective threads, training resources published by teams (1678 Citrus Circuits, Spectrum 3847, 1114 Simbotics, 148/JVN) and FIRST-adjacent articles.

## 1. Games that required the widest variety of mechanisms

### 2022 Rapid React
Rapid React is the community's consensus "full-stack mechanism game." In ["What did Rapid React get RIGHT?"](https://www.chiefdelphi.com/t/what-did-rapid-react-get-right/410496), Karthik Kanagasabapathy (1114 strategy mentor) called it "one of the best FRC games of all time," noting "the tasks in the game led to a large variety of robot designs." A competitive Rapid React robot needed a ground intake, an indexer or serializer, a flywheel shooter (optionally turreted), a drivetrain and a traversal climber. User crueter noted "I saw several dozen climbs and I don't think there were two identical climbs at all," and that the game let "low-resource/skill teams build competitive robots... with more advanced teams opting for the high end stuff, like windmill/otherwise very fast climbs, turrets." In the ["Best game for practice CAD"](https://www.chiefdelphi.com/t/best-game-for-practice-cad/434157) thread, student designer Takanori recommended it for training: "the packaging around the climber, shooter, intakes, transfer/magazine, and electronics really made me think. I'd recommend Rapid React 100%."

### 2020/2021 Infinite Recharge
Infinite Recharge demanded the longest single-robot subsystem list of the modern era (intake, indexer, turreted shooter, vision targeting, buddy-less climber, control-panel spinner), but mentors consider it too much. In the Rapid React thread, theIme wrote "Games like Infinite Recharge were too complicated... I still don't really know the point of the color wheel, and I was a FRC member for that season."

### 2025 Reefscape
Reefscape is the most recent "forced tradeoffs" game. In the [Reefscape complexity poll](https://www.chiefdelphi.com/t/poll-reefscape-as-the-most-complex-game-in-recent-memory/483702), Ryan_Swanson wrote: "This game is definitely fun from a robot design perspective. There are forced tradeoffs with every strategic decision. Even if your team can do everything you'll still be required to make performance limiting sacrifices." Eliot noted that almost no robot could do everything (L1–L4 coral, net algae, ground intake for both pieces, deep climb) under the weight limit. Reefscape robots combined elevators, articulated end effectors, ground intakes, algae mechanisms and deep-climb winches, probably the widest manipulator variety of 2022–2025.

### 2018 Power Up
Power Up is repeatedly cited as the best training game because its mechanisms recur in later games. In the CAD-practice thread, mentor pevvvvvvvvv wrote: "I used to use 2018 as a practice exercise for my newbies! The sort of mechanisms it uses (elevator, roller claw, rung climber) have been recycled a lot in recent games."

### 2023 Charged Up
Charged Up produced more design diversity than expected for a pick-and-place game. In [The Charged Up Retrospective](https://www.chiefdelphi.com/t/the-charged-up-retrospective/443693), e172 wrote: "There were a surprising amount of ways to pick-and-place game elements... This led to more variability among robots, and not settling on a clear-cut top concept like [1323's] cube intake in 2018."

## 2. Low floor, high ceiling

FIRST designs for this on purpose. A 2013 [Game Design Committee Q&A thread](https://www.chiefdelphi.com/t/faf-june-28-2013-game-design-committee/130154) confirms that the GDC "attempts to create games that have a low floor but a high ceiling... tasks that very basic robots can support, along with tasks that require more sophisticated designs."

### Examples
- 2022 Rapid React: the low goal and low climb rungs were meaningful for rookies, and traversal climbs and turrets challenged elite teams. Karthik: "All tasks looked hard to the audience, but were very achievable for teams of all levels, while still providing appropriate engineering challenges." gavandavis292: "You just need some drive practice, a half decent intake, an accurate shooter, and some sort of climb to be a little bit competitive."
- 2024 Crescendo: the amp made the "easy" task strategically essential (kindagood in [Crescendo vs Reefscape](https://www.chiefdelphi.com/t/crescendo-vs-reefscape/503365)). The 2024 KitBot (per the [FRC Blog KitBot threads](https://www.chiefdelphi.com/t/frc-blog-looking-back-on-the-2024-kitbot-moving-forward/469042)) let rookie teams shoot into the speaker on day one. One rookie kitbot team became an 8th-seed alliance captain, and the number of pushbots dropped visibly. This is the strongest recent intervention to raise the floor.
- 2023 Charged Up: Leap called it "the game Recycle Rush should have been. Everyone had a way to participate in a meaningful way which was great for teams all across the spectrum." Pizzatc: cubes made it "possible for lower level teams to still put up high scores."

### Counter-examples
- 2025 Reefscape had a nominal floor (L1 trough, processor, Everybot archetype), but mentors found it deceptive. s-neff in [Favorite FRC Game (2025 Updated)](https://www.chiefdelphi.com/t/favorite-frc-game-2025-updated/504677): "Absolutely brutal robot design challenge, everything that looks easy is hard, lots of frustrated teenagers to manage." Lucs100 in [Review of Reefscape](https://www.chiefdelphi.com/t/review-of-reefscape/499842): "At any level of play below the very top the bottom falls out pretty quickly. Strong teams can cycle to L4 several times faster than weaker teams can cycle to L1... We are talking about essentially completely different games between the different 'tiers' of teams." The ceiling was high, but the floor was lower than it looked.
- 2015 Recycle Rush: Ryan_Swanson's [Recycle Rush Reflection](https://www.chiefdelphi.com/t/a-recycle-rush-reflection/143576) says "the flaws become more evident at the lower levels." Matches at weak regionals were dead, while deep fields (Midwest) were exciting; the floor was a cliff.
- 2019 Deep Space required precise placement of two game pieces under heavy defense. The [5987 Throwback thread](https://www.chiefdelphi.com/t/frc-throwback-series-destination-deep-space-2019-strategy-lessons-top-3-robots/507936) notes that the top teams (973, 3538) "built something relatively simple and executed well." It is a good teaching case for elegance over feature count.

### Perceived and actual difficulty
The sharpest educational framing found comes from s-neff (a mentor, in Crescendo vs Reefscape): "I loved that Crescendo had 'looks easy is easy' (single chain climbing), 'looks hard is easy' (scoring notes) and 'looks hard is hard' tasks (trap). I was frustrated that Reefscape algae scoring and deep climb was 'looks easy is hard'... The program is most inspirational when perceived and actual difficulty are aligned, or when actual difficulty is lower than perceived difficulty." It is the clearest design-for-education principle in the sources reviewed.

## 3. Design lessons mentors attribute to each recent game (2022–2025)

### 2022 Rapid React: fundamentals after COVID
Coming out of COVID, mentors valued its limited scope. Lil_Lavery: "an appropriate-level of engineering challenge to come back from a season with no robots... Tasks we had seen before with a twist. Readily available solutions demonstrated in the very recent past." TGilmore533: "Instead of throwing them to the wolves, it gave teams a chance to regroup and get back to the basics." The game taught projectile physics and flywheel tuning, ball-path serialization, and, through the traversal climb, linkage and hook design and sequencing (climb state machines).

### 2023 Charged Up: kinematics, superstructures and the limits of optimization
Charged Up taught arm-versus-elevator tradeoff analysis, double-jointed arm control (for many teams, a first use of feedforward on multi-DOF arms) and compliance in cone and cube end effectors. Michael_Corsetto (1678 design mentor) summarized it: "Many teams could score something / Endgame too easy / Diminishing returns scoring / Not much interaction with opponent... Bonus: 4ft extensions = very unsafe. Never again." Leap's critique doubles as a strategy lesson: "Regional winners were determined in Build Season... This was a game about optimization... this game could have been run in a simulation with very little difference in outcome."

### 2024 Crescendo: strategy, alliance play and vision
Crescendo is widely called the most strategically educational recent game. art3misfowel: "Crescendo is a mechanically simple game, with basic robot strategy, but insanely in depth game strategy." It taught on-the-fly auto-aim (the first full AprilTag season brought shoot-while-moving and pose estimation within reach of mid-tier teams), passing plays, the amp/speaker economy, and switching roles between matches. In [Thoughts on Crescendo a few weeks in](https://www.chiefdelphi.com/t/thoughts-on-crescendo-a-few-weeks-in/460982), benmoeller wrote: "Being good at both [speaker and amp] adds a ton of value," compared with earlier years in which "you can be really good at one thing and do fine." The cautionary lessons are seg9585's "The trap is a trap. Basically worthless in elims compared to its design difficulty" (a cost-benefit scoping lesson) and ChiefK33f's complaint that mid-season "meta" rebuilds made "strategic design useless" at the top.

### 2025 Reefscape: tradeoff engineering and software
Darre: "The biggest positive for this game is the design challenge; so many teams had to make a sacrifice in one way or another. Even the top teams had to make design cuts until the optimal archetype revealed itself a la ground intake." Thor5090: "Reefscape definitely tested a team's ability to program vs. building a robot. Without the ability to incorporate vision with precision auto alignment teams were significantly at a disadvantage." No recent game pushed harder on teaching reef-relative pose control and auto-align. MrPotato noted that the meta shifted in a healthy direction: "this year L1 and algae became more important as teams got better" (unlike Charged Up, which "felt 'solved' before teams even got to Houston"). Richard_Wallace: "Iteration is the soul of innovation. This game challenged all of us to iterate right up to the end."

## 4. What makes an offseason design challenge effective for teaching

The community-run challenges show these patterns.

### A tight scope that simulates build season
The [1678 Storm Surge CADathon](https://www.chiefdelphi.com/t/1678-storm-surge-cadathon/396100) ran 9 days across two weekends, with judging criteria withheld until kickoff ("DO NOT START... Crucial judging criteria that will fundamentally determine the design of your robot will not be released before then"). Entrants therefore had to design from requirements from the start. Team-size caps (3 members across teams) prevented all-star stacking.

### Deliverables beyond CAD
Storm Surge required CAD files, renders and a written submission form. The [Pack Challenge](https://www.chiefdelphi.com/t/pack-challenge-a-real-world-offseason-cad-project-for-frc-teams/501254) (a palletizer design backed by PMMI) requires a 5-minute video "that communicates your design solution," design documentation and optional renders, with the stated aim of building "CAD, documentation, and presentation skills."

### Public expert feedback
Storm Surge ended with a live judged Twitch show and a panel of 1678 mentors and students. The Pack Challenge advertises "feedback from real industry engineers" and a professional-style design review. Participant kogappa (LigerBots 2877) posted a full design rationale afterward, which suggests the format prompts reflective writeups. That team cut the high goal after a points-per-cycle analysis, a small-scale lesson in scoping.

### Choosing the practice game
In the CAD-practice thread, mentors agree on three options: pick a game whose mechanisms transfer (2018, 2020, 2022); use a purpose-built CADathon game (yadin: "you can compare your designs to the 'best' designs from that CADathon"); or copy an elite team's robot (howlongismyname: "Copying bots lets you... learn the intent of how things are designed"). Nick.kremer's general advice is that motivation matters more than curriculum: "simply attempting something will bring you a wealth of experience."

### Hands-on scaffolding before CAD
In [Hands On FRC Design Training](https://www.chiefdelphi.com/t/hands-on-frc-design-training/415046), Nick.kremer recommends physical prototyping systems (Spectrum's [ProtoPipe](https://spectrum3847.org/protopipe-rapid-prototyping-system/), HypeBlocks, TTB blocks) "so they can focus on what to build not how to build it." Others add "mock kickoffs" using old reveal videos (LuiTheFly) and 2D paper-linkage climber exercises (sgeckler). In [Design Challenges](https://www.chiefdelphi.com/t/design-challenges/506815), a design lead reports that teaching CAD through Lego re-modeling is faster than tool-by-tool lessons.

### Institutional programs
1678 publishes [Fall Workshops](https://www.citruscircuits.org/fallworkshops.html) and [training resources](https://www.citruscircuits.org/training-resources) (seven subteam tracks, recorded lectures including Corsetto's Strategic Design series). Spectrum 3847 runs elective skill camps and new-student Everybot/MCC robot builds so rookies "build, wire, and program a robot before the design for their actual robot is complete" ([Spectrum blog](http://blog.spectrum3847.org/2020/01/2020-day-14-controls-and-everybot.html)). JVN's [build journals](https://johnvneun.com/jvns-robots) model open daily design reasoning ("There are no failures, only lessons"). Karthik's [Effective FIRST Strategies](https://www.simbotics.org/resources/strategy) remains the standard strategy-education deck.

## 5. Game mechanics that caused student disengagement

### No alliance interaction, or a solved game
Recycle Rush (2015) is the archetype: "There was no energy from the crowd... I heard the same message over and over: 'This game is boring'" (Ryan_Swanson). Orthofort adds a point that matters for educators: the game "appeals more to the actual people involved with the robot." It was a strong design challenge and a dead competition, and retention suffers when only the design students are engaged. Charged Up drew the same critique in milder form (Leap: "could have been run in a simulation").

### Tasks that look easy but are hard
Reefscape's algae and deep climb (s-neff: "lots of frustrated teenagers to manage") and its tier gaps that weaker teams could not close (Lucs100) demoralize mid-tier students.

### Inexplicable or dead mechanics
Examples are Infinite Recharge's control panel (theIme: "I still don't really know the point of the color wheel"), Crescendo's trap and random microphone scoring (seg9585), and Reefscape's processor, which several posters describe as dead after quals. Mechanisms that students build but never meaningfully use teach cynicism.

### Frequent robot damage
Crescendo's open-field collisions: "We did a robot rebuild... because our robot was destroyed... that's drained my whole team to the dregs. A game where robot damage is so pervasive is a bad game" (anon, Thoughts-on-Crescendo thread).

### Matches decided by fouls and refereeing variance
Stronghold "will be remembered as the year of fouls... the finals of Einstein were decided on a foul" ([Stronghold thread](https://www.chiefdelphi.com/t/what-will-frc-2016-stronghold-be-remembered-by/151928)). Knufire on Rapid React: "most of the gripes... are with its refereeing and not the game design."

### All-or-nothing endgames
Steamworks' climb (nuclearnerd: "if you missed a climb the whole effort was wasted"; Kevin_Leonard: teams "won/lost matches at random... based entirely on whether their partners climbed"). The 2017 fuel shooting was also nearly impossible for average teams.

## Synthesis

For an educational offseason challenge, the community evidence points to the following:
- Use a Rapid React or Power Up-class mechanism set (intake, serializer, shooter or placer, climber) for breadth with achievable physics.
- Borrow Crescendo's strategy layer, a low-skill task that stays strategically vital, for engagement.
- Borrow Reefscape's forced-tradeoff structure for depth in design thinking, but follow s-neff's alignment principle and make perceived difficulty match actual difficulty.
- Package it like the 1678 and Pack Challenge model: a fixed short window, criteria revealed at kickoff, deliverables of CAD plus a written rationale and a short video, and a public review show judged by experts.
