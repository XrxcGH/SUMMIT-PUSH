## Verdict
Greenlight FIRST ASCENT: it is the only concept with a documented kitbot path and a true graduation ladder, its s-neff discipline is airtight (every easy task looks easy, the Summit looks exactly as hard as it is), the Forecast keeps the meta rotating all season, and its field is the cheapest and cleanest to reproduce — the safest bet for keeping mid-tier students engaged for a full CADathon while still forking elite archetypes. HIGHBALL is the close second and arguably the richer pure-design exercise (the floppy mailbag handle and relay handoff are the most novel mechanism problems in the field), but its far-side-train sightline problem quietly taxes exactly the rookies its floor claims to serve, and relay refereeing plus the five-car field build add risk a mentor-run offseason event doesn't need; adopt its starting-line-negotiation auto and 3/8/15 swinging-bar endgame ideas into FIRST ASCENT if possible. MAINLINE has the best interaction model but stakes the whole game on accrual tuning — a single-point-of-failure balance problem no CADathon has playtest bandwidth to solve. SUMMIT SIGNAL has the best hook but the narrowest mechanism debate (everything is a vertical drop-on), and RELIQUARY's six-faces-one-structure traffic is a foul magnet waiting to happen. If running FIRST ASCENT, execute fixes 1 and 2 (socket tolerance prototype, Expedition RP lockout softening) before publishing the manual.

## Details
### FIRST ASCENT
- Strengths: The most honest floor of the five: a PUBLISHED kitbot path (crates-only, depot + 24-in shelf) with an explicit graduation ladder (add wrist -> sockets, add elevator -> mid tier, add hooks -> Ledge) is exactly how you keep mid-tier students engaged instead of demoralized. Ruthless s-neff discipline everywhere — whole-inch dimensions, only 15/30/45-deg angles, easy tasks that look easy, a Summit that looks as brutal as it is. The Forecast rotates the doubled Storm Tier so the meta never freezes, Camp bonuses make mixed alliances structurally outvalue monoculture (the anti-2015 design), and the 15-deg inclined, laterally staggered Headwall is the freshest climb geometry in the field — a genuine traversal with a no-cliff 3/12/20/30 ladder in front of the crowd. Buildability is superb (crag <$180, single-lane headwall, single-speed-square angles). For a CADathon, the elevator-vs-arm decision is genuinely contested by the geometry (flat shelves vs 30-deg sockets vs 45-deg pegs vs 78-in ceiling), which generates real student debates with numbers.
- Concerns: Interaction is the weakest axis: scoring is own-crag only, so all cross-alliance play is defense and the contested Center Cache — less counterplay than MAINLINE's territory war. Expedition RP can be mathematically locked out by qual-schedule luck if you draw no O2/ring partner. Summit traversal may see <10% success in weeks 1-2. O2 socket tolerance (0.75 in/side) is one field-build variance away from an s-neff violation. Forecast needs an FMS-free fallback for offseason play.
- Fixes: 1) Prototype the socket ID and the 12-in rung stagger BEFORE freezing dimensions; publish socket ID as a strictly toleranced field dimension with a +/- callout. 2) Soften Expedition lockout: let a correctly-typed Depot piece satisfy the Camp I slot for that type, or add a lower 'Camp I via any 3 pieces on the low band' alternative at Regionals only. 3) Publish the offseason Forecast fallback now (card draw + manual LED) so practice fields don't degrade to scripted autos. 4) Add a bumper-height-only contact rule within 3 ft of crag faces to curb tip-over hunting of high-CG robots at the 78-in pegs. 5) Give the defender role a carrot — e.g., a small denial-relevant neutral respawn at the Center Cache — so the third-robot debate has a real defensive branch.

### HIGHBALL
- Strengths: The richest design-tradeoff space of the five for a CADathon: rigid cube + rolling barrel + floppy handled bag is a triple-geometry intake problem nobody solves at 115 lb, the mailbag-handle-to-15-deg-peg presentation is a genuinely novel end-effector problem, and the Mail Relay creates real passing plays (sports-team drive practice, not just cycling). Best auto of the field — starting-line negotiation in queue, 6-way manifest branching, and vision-mandatory placement are three interlocking decisions, not a script. The 3/8/15 free-swinging Brakeman's Bar with damping as the mastery gate is textbook 2022-traversal design. Count-the-lamps legibility is excellent, and the FULL CONSIST RP consuming the same scoring actions (just aimed) is sophisticated RP design.
- Concerns: The far-side train is the game's structural flaw: every scoring action, including the 'easy' caboose push, happens 45+ ft from your own glass through crossing traffic — the task is easy but the context isn't, a partial s-neff violation for weak-vision rookies that the authors only half-mitigate. Mail Relay possession tracking is a protest magnet unless wording is airtight. The 16-ft five-car train is the biggest field build here (~$600). Mailbag handle is the single most-abused feature and needs a torn-handle rule. Six autos crossing midfield is unsimulated collision risk.
- Fixes: 1) Give each alliance one near-side low-tier outlet — a small 'siding' or transfer platform on its own half worth caboose-level points — so a sightline-limited rookie has a close option all match. 2) Replace judgment-based relay possession with a mechanical definition: relay credit if the bag is exchanged robot-to-robot with both bumpers inside a taped RELAY ZONE band at midfield (zone-based, binary, one ref). 3) Widen gantry stations to 54-60 in now, before field drawings, not after week zero. 4) Run the six-robot auto traffic sim and, if needed, stagger trackside start boxes so opposing trackside robots face away from each other. 5) Spec the mailbag handle to Crescendo-lessons abuse standards (bar-tack pull test number in the manual) plus an in-match replacement rule.

### MAINLINE
- Strengths: The best cross-alliance interaction in the field: territory ownership with additive-only counterplay means every placement is simultaneously offense and denial, and the back-to-back face geometry gets you contested space without intake-vs-intake scrums. The kitbot floor is strategically real — a pushed ground-bay crate carries Signal Weight, so the simplest robot on the field is fighting the territory war, not doing a consolation task. Capacity-locking creates genuine 'bearing-off' endgame-of-territory math. Cheapest, simplest field build of the five (~$250, 6 sheets). The lever-clack moment is a legitimately great signature.
- Concerns: Accrual-vs-placement balance is a single point of failure the authors themselves name: if pts/sec runs even slightly hot this becomes 'one lantern bot decides matches' — Power Up's exact disease — and per-second drift is inherently less watchable than pieces landing (scoreboard moves without visible events; the paddles help but don't fully fix it). The 4.5-ft single-robot chokepoint lanes are a pin-and-foul factory that will demoralize mid-tier drivers, and G-rules around 'contact while placing' at depots will be brutal to referee. Additive-only locking creates mathematically dead depots mid-match. Two currencies (points + Signal Weight + accrual) is a heavy cognitive load for students deriving robot requirements.
- Fixes: 1) Quantize accrual into discrete 5-second ticks displayed as physical/LED pips on each structure — easier to tune, easier to watch, easier to protest-proof. 2) Collapse the double currency: make Signal Weight equal the piece's point value so students track one number. 3) Widen chokepoint lanes to 6 ft (shrink depot width) and add an explicit no-pin zone within 3 ft of depot faces. 4) Build the mid-match unlock into v1, not a contingency: a second shelf row (or +4 bay capacity) unlocks at T-60 so locked depots come back to life for the endgame push. 5) Run the lantern-bot dominance playtest first — one elite lantern specialist vs a balanced trio — and tune the 72-in lantern weight before anything else.

### SUMMIT SIGNAL
- Strengths: The single best spectator moment of all five concepts: beacons physically igniting up the mountainside is an instantly legible, marketing-grade hook, and stacked posts make score state countable from the stands. 'Any piece on a post scores for that post's alliance' is elegant exploit-killing rules writing. The First Light auto (nine paths, preload negotiation in queue) and the Cell-scarcity economy with beacon-ordering as a studied skill are genuinely deep. Trimmed traffic cones as a game piece is inspired buildability.
- Concerns: Mechanism monoculture is the teaching weakness: nearly every scoring action is a vertical drop-on, so the elevator wins the elevator-vs-arm debate almost by default and the CADathon tradeoff space narrows — three piece geometries but essentially one approach vector. Flare capping at 76-82 in is the precision ceiling holding up the entire Network RP; if the chamfer funnel isn't as forgiving as hoped, the game's signature mechanic dies and mid-tier teams are frustrated at height. The floor is thinner than claimed — the 40-in hook 'kitbot task' still needs a fixed arm, and there is no documented kitbot path. Ring-lobbing onto low posts could erode pick-and-place primacy. Seat-sensor LEDs are a field-reliability single point of failure in the game's most important moment.
- Fixes: 1) Diversify approach geometry: convert the Mid Tower to a side-load shelf or angled cradle so arms have a real constituency and the architecture debate reopens. 2) Prototype the flare socket now; if capping success for a well-built mid-tier arm is under ~70%, enlarge the socket to 3.5-4 in and deepen the tip chamfer before freeze. 3) Write the launch-distance rule into v1 rather than waiting for the lobbing meta. 4) Publish an explicit kitbot path document (drivetrain + one fixed hook arm: depot, 38-in posts, signal hooks, shelter park) FIRST-ASCENT-style. 5) Make the ref-visible fallback primary: beacon = flare seated over ring+cell by rule, LEDs decorative, so a dead sensor never touches the score.

### RELIQUARY
- Strengths: The vault slot is the single best rookie task in any of the five concepts — floor-level, orientation-free, 8-in target, feeds the entry RP AND the Champs stretch RP, and is exactly as trivial as it looks. The urn is the most novel piece here (tip control, neck gripping, partial credit when tipped — a real CAD problem no recent FRC game has posed). The coin-column thermometers are good legibility, team-version-one-face buildability is smart, and the Curator's Bonus tri-piece auto forces the same healthy alliance negotiation as the best concepts.
- Concerns: Six scoring faces on one central structure is the field's riskiest traffic architecture: all twelve robot-minutes of scoring converge on a 6-ft hexagon, and the 12-in Curation Zone will be the most-protested rule at every event — this is the G415 trap the concept claims to avoid. Per-face capacity is tight (1 pedestal, 1 plinth, 1 easel, 3/peg), so elite alliances may saturate the high-value board mid-match and the game decays into vault grinding plus endgame. The endgame is the most generic of the five — a competent but anonymous 3-tier offset climb with no signature moment. Urn frustration could make the piece a museum piece in quals.
- Fixes: 1) Grow the rotunda to 8 ft across flats and make the protection rule bumper-geometric (no contact with a robot whose bumpers break a taped arc) rather than a 12-in judgment call. 2) Raise per-face capacity — 2 plinth positions and 4-deep pegs — so elites don't exhaust the scoring surface by T-60. 3) Give the endgame an identity: put a small artifact-placement action at the top of the high rung (cap the scaffold with a medallion for +5) to fuse the climb with the museum theme. 4) Add a mid-value urn cradle at ~12 in between tipped-partial and pedestal so mid-tier teams have an urn stepping stone. 5) Pre-commit the Masterpiece fallback (1 face + 8 vault coins) as a published week-3 trigger condition, not a maybe.
