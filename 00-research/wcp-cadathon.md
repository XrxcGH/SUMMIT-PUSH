# The WCP CADathon

## Key takeaways
- The WCP CADathon is a week-long annual offseason FRC design competition. WCP releases a fictional game (a full FRC-style manual, Onshape field CAD and a score calculator, plus a MoSim Unity simulator in 2025); teams CAD a robot and write a tech binder in ~7 days; judges rank the public submissions weeks later.
- There have been two editions: DunkTank (Oct 2024, won by 8033 Highlander Robotics) and Hero Heist (Nov 2025, 100+ entries, won by 5458 Digital Minds). Hero Heist's three robot classes (Commander/Mystic/Gadgeteer) forced a strategic choice before design began, which participants found compelling.
- The deliverables are two items: robot CAD (Onshape required in 2025) and a PDF tech binder covering strategy, robot function and engineering rationale. Both are posted publicly to the Chief Delphi thread, which creates a shared library of 100+ designs.
- Judging rewards strategy-first design. The criteria are the quality of the strategic analysis, feasibility and practicality (judged within a team's resource capacity), the effectiveness of the documentation, and a bonus for creative use of WCP parts. Winner citations consistently emphasize points modeling and decision rationale over CAD polish.
- Team rules: max 2 entries per FRC team, 1 mentor per 2 students, and students expected to do at least half the work (honor system). Cross-team collaborations, solo entries and alumni are common and accepted. Q&A runs directly through the CD thread, with the game designer (Andrew Lawrence) answering.
- The top prize is tied to team identity: 1st place gets a TeamWCP sponsorship (passed down if a past winner repeats). Other prizes are WCP/Fabworks gift cards, a Bambu P1S for People's Choice and (2024) a Best Onshape Submission award with a PTC grant.
- The community-run F4 CADathon (12+ events, sponsored by WCP) established the format and its best practices: formal Q&A forms with public answer sheets, Team Updates during the event, team-size caps for fairness, and a live results show with ranked feedback on FUN's Twitch, which is still the benchmark for transparent judging.
- The main participant frustrations with WCP's version are judging delays (2025 results slipped a month and landed 3 days before kickoff; People's Choice was still unannounced 7 months later) and the decision to drop individual feedback in 2025 'due to bandwidth' after providing it in 2024.
- What participants say makes a CADathon good: complete release materials of kickoff quality, fast and authoritative rules Q&A, transparent judges and criteria, individual feedback, public submissions, a ~1-week window in November, and games with real strategic depth. What erodes trust: unnamed judges, vague criteria, no limits on resources, mentor-stacked teams competing against students, and ambiguous scoring rules (Hero Heist's district-ownership wording briefly implied a 20-30 point exploit).
- Other offseason activities differ. Ri3D is a 72-hour physical build after the real kickoff (a demonstration that is not judged); Behind the Bumpers is FUN's robot interview series (content, not a challenge). Many teams (226, 271) now use the WCP CADathon as the capstone of their rookie design training, supported by the frcdesign.org/David's Design Server community.
- Timeline design matters. F4's original 3-day format was permanently extended to 1 week, and a November window (after offseason competitions, before build season) was credited with boosting participation compared with early October.

## 1. Overview

The WCP CADathon is an annual, week-long offseason FRC design competition run by WestCoast Products (WCP), one of the major FRC COTS vendors. Teams receive a fictional FRC-style game (with a full game manual and field CAD) and have roughly one week to design a complete robot in CAD and document their strategy and engineering decisions. Judges evaluate the submissions and announce winners a few weeks later. WCP's stated goal for its first event was "to connect with the community in a fun and exciting way" ([WCP CADathon 2024: DunkTank — Chief Delphi](https://www.chiefdelphi.com/t/wcp-cadathon-2024-dunktank/472262); [wcproducts.com/pages/cadathon](https://wcproducts.com/pages/cadathon)).

The CADathon concept predates WCP. The community-run F4 CADathon series (12+ events, 2017–2022) popularized the format and defined it as "like a hackathon but with CAD. People/teams are given a short period of time to CAD a specific thing (in this case a robot) ending with a panel of judges selecting a winner" ([9th F4 CADathon — Rolling Rampage](https://www.chiefdelphi.com/t/9th-f4-cadathon-rolling-rampage-rankings-archives-available/386821)). Andrew Lawrence (Andrew_L on CD), who designed both WCP CADathon games, was active in the earlier CADathon community, and the WCP events are in effect a professionally run successor to F4's community events.

There have been two WCP CADathons so far:
- 2024: "DunkTank" (first edition, October 2024)
- 2025: "Hero Heist" (second edition, November 2025)

## 2. Timeline, teams and organization

### Timeline (both years)
| Phase | DunkTank 2024 | Hero Heist 2025 |
|---|---|---|
| Announcement/theme reveal | 9/30/24 | 11/1/25 |
| Game reveal (start) | 10/4/24, 12pm PST | 11/10/25, ~1–2pm PT |
| Submission deadline | 10/13/24, 11:59pm PST | 11/17/25, 11:59pm PST |
| Results (scheduled) | 11/1/24 | 12/3/25 |
| Results (actual) | ~11/22/24 | 1/6/26 (delayed) |

The design window is about one week (2024's was slightly longer, ~9 days). Results follow a judging period of several weeks. In 2025 judging slipped: results scheduled for Dec 3 arrived Jan 6, days before FRC kickoff, and the People's Choice winner still had not been announced by mid-2026. The delay became a running joke in the thread ("Quarter of the way to the next WCP CADATHON watching this thread hoping for the peoples choice award") ([Hero Heist thread](https://www.chiefdelphi.com/t/wcp-2025-cadathon-hero-heist/507753)).

### Team formation
Per the 2025 rules on WCP's site: max 2 CADathon teams per FRC team, no more than 1 mentor per 2 high-school students, and students are expected (honor system) to do at least half the work. Teams register through a Google Form and can register at any time during the event. Multiple submissions per organization were allowed in 2025 (R.C: "Submit as much as you'd like"). Cross-team collaborations are common and accepted (for example "Team JPC," a collaboration of designers from 2035/4159/9038/9470), and solo entries by individuals or alumni are welcome. 8033's winning 2024 entry openly credited helpers from teams 1153, 9442 and 111.

F4's team rules were stricter about fairness: unlimited team size only if all members were from the same FIRST team, and otherwise a maximum of 3 members, "to prevent the 10 best CADathon participants from 10 different teams from teaming up." Solo entrants could request random partner matching by CAD software and timezone ([9th F4 CADathon](https://www.chiefdelphi.com/t/9th-f4-cadathon-rolling-rampage-rankings-archives-available/386821)).

### Communication channels
WCP runs the official Q&A through the Chief Delphi thread itself. The game designer (Andrew_L) answers rules questions directly in the thread ("this forum post is the best place to ask game questions"). F4 instead used a Discord server (migrated from Slack), a formal Q&A Google Form with a public sheet of answered questions, and published Team Updates (rule revisions during the event, as in real FRC). The MoSim project maintains its own Discord for the simulator integration.

## 3. Release materials

The WCP game reveal package is modeled on an FRC kickoff:
- A full game manual with FRC-style numbered rules (G-rules, R-rules), match phases (auto/teleop/endgame, 2:15 matches in DunkTank), scoring tables and robot constraints (frame perimeter; extension limits such as "G08: horizontal dimensions may never exceed 18" beyond FRAME PERIMETER"; possession limits; weight limits; current-season bumper rules).
- Field CAD (an Onshape document; F4 provided STEP + Onshape).
- A score calculator spreadsheet (praised in 2024: "Huge fan of the linked core calculator. This is something FIRST could easily do").
- New in 2025, MoSimBuilder Alpha integration: a Unity-based simulation in which teams could play the game with simple robot archetypes before designing, described as "the KrayonCAD of FRC video games" ([Hero Heist reveal post](https://www.chiefdelphi.com/t/wcp-2025-cadathon-hero-heist/507753)).

### The games
- DunkTank (2024) had a basketball theme. Robots dunk BASKETBALLS (possession limited to 1 at a time) and collect ELECTROLYTES (unlimited possession) to boost performance, with "hoop stacks" and clearing mechanics. The community liked the *Idiocracy*-flavored theme ("sponsored by BRAWNDO, the thirst mutilator").
- Hero Heist (2025) had a superhero theme, set in "Corgiopolis." Teams designed a robot for one of three classes (COMMANDER, MYSTIC, GADGETEER), each with different rules (e.g., extension limits), and competed for FAME by controlling DISTRICTS with SPEECH BUBBLES (balls) and STORY PANELS (HDPE panels), plus climbs. The class system forced a strategic choice before design and produced lively meta-analysis (CD polls on which class would be most and least common). The Commander class proved unpopular, and one solo entrant chose to design one for that reason.

## 4. Deliverables

Submissions require two artifacts (2025):
1. Robot CAD: an Onshape link was required in 2025 ("CRITICAL NOTE: All submissions must be made using OnShape"); 2024 allowed Onshape or STEP.
2. Documentation, the "tech binder" (PDF): robot function, strategic analysis and the rationale for engineering decisions.

All submissions (CAD + documentation) must be posted publicly to the Chief Delphi thread, which turns the deadline into a large public design share, one of the event's most valued features. Renders and reveal-style videos are common but optional (5414 Pearadox made a full reveal video for its "suPEARman" design). F4 also required native CAD files, renders uploaded to a GrabCAD Workbench, and a Google Forms "pit scouting document."

Participation: 2024 drew enough entries that WCP ran multi-round judging with 3 winners + 8 named finalists (254, 604, 1902, 5987, 7475, etc.). 2025 had "well over 100" submissions, with 31 teams advancing to Round 2 (including 254, 971, 111, 2767, 6800, 1540) ([results post, Jan 6, 2026](https://www.chiefdelphi.com/t/wcp-2025-cadathon-hero-heist/507753?page=12)).

## 5. Judging criteria and process

Per WCP's CADathon page, judging evaluates:
- Strategic analysis quality (does the design follow from a coherent reading of the game and its meta?)
- Design feasibility and practicality ("teams judged within their resource capacity")
- Documentation effectiveness
- Creative use of WCP components (a bonus only; WCP parts are not mandatory)

The judges' citations in the winners' posts show what wins. 2024 winner 8033's "Whirlpool" was praised for mechanism detail (a torsion-spring/cable hopper flap), for documentation "offering thorough breakdowns and clear explanations of their decision-making process," and for Onshape best practices (driving sketches, organized features, high-fidelity block CAD before detail work). 2025 winner 5458 was cited for "fast, repeatable cycles and endgame consistency… a strong understanding of ownership mechanics… while staying mechanically cohesive." Strategy-first design with clear points modeling recurs in every citation; CAD polish without strategic justification does not reach the top of the rankings.

Process: 2024 used named judges (6 listed, including Andrew Lawrence) and sent individual feedback to every team. 2025 used two judging rounds, and WCP announced it would not send individual feedback "due to our current bandwidth," a step back from 2024 (see §7). In F4's process, submissions were split in thirds among 3 judges, each judge picked a top 5–7, and then all judges reviewed those, so "the top 15 will have feedback from all 3 judges." Results were delivered as a live ranked show on FIRST Updates Now's Twitch channel with recorded feedback for each rank (archived on YouTube for ranks 1–5, 6–15, 16–62).

### Prizes
2025: 1st = TeamWCP sponsorship for the upcoming season (a repeat winner passes the sponsorship down so new teams enter #TeamWCP), 2nd = $250 WCP gift card, 3rd = $150; Fabworks gift cards ($500/$350/$150); People's Choice = Bambu Lab P1S printer; swag for Best Class Design. 2024 also had a Best Onshape Submission award (won by 8033) that carried a guaranteed PTC grant. Partners: Fabworks, Onshape/PTC, MoSim.

## 6. Other FRC offseason design challenges

- **F4 CADathon (2017–2022, ~12 events):** the community original. It normally ran for 3 days (extended to 1 week from 2020), twice a year, free and open to all ages. It had formal Q&A forms, Team Updates, GrabCAD submission, a live Twitch results show with feedback for each team, and prizes sponsored by WCP. Its live feedback show is still the benchmark for transparent judging ([2020 Spring Special results](https://www.chiefdelphi.com/t/2020-spring-special-f4-cadathon-results-posted/383527)).
- **WRRF WaterWorks CADathon (2020):** a first attempt by a regional organization, with 1 week of design + 1 week of judging, teams of ≤3, and CAD + PDF documentation judged on "function, aesthetic, feasibility, and documentation" with category awards scored by points. It is remembered mostly for the public criticism it received over vague judging details (see §7) ([WRRF WaterWorks CADathon](https://www.chiefdelphi.com/t/wrrf-waterworks-cadathon/388027)).
- **Robot in 3 Days (Ri3D):** the physical-build counterpart. Collegiate teams build a working robot in 72 hours after the real FRC kickoff and document everything to help teams start their season. It is a demonstration and educational sprint without judging; CADathons are its offseason, design-only counterpart, with fictional games and judged rankings ([Ri3D category on CD](https://www.chiefdelphi.com/c/other/robot-in-3-days-ri3d/79); [AndyMark Ri3D](https://andymark.com/pages/meet-the-ri3d-teams)).
- **Behind the Bumpers (FUN Robotics Network):** an in-season robot-interview video series (with short "Pit Stop" companions) and not a challenge. It is relevant as the design-communication genre that CADathon tech binders imitate, and FUN historically hosted F4's results shows ([Behind the Bumpers thread](https://www.chiefdelphi.com/t/behind-the-bumpers-pit-stop-interviews-frc-rebuilt/515918)).
- **Team-internal offseason design challenges:** many teams now use the WCP CADathon as the capstone of rookie training. 226 Hammerheads had its design rookies build "STARSCREAM" for Hero Heist using 3005's high-fidelity block-CAD technique ([226 Fall Recap](https://www.chiefdelphi.com/t/226-hammerheads-fall-project-recap/510230)); 271 used it to move the team onto Onshape.
- **The wider community:** winners repeatedly credit frcdesign.org and David's Design Server with building the CAD-training community that feeds CADathon participation.

## 7. Participant views

### What participants praise
- A complete kickoff experience: a full FRC-style manual, field CAD, a score calculator and (2025) a playable simulator. Teams call it "the tune-up we needed" and "a fantastic way to get people engaged in the offseason" ([DunkTank thread](https://www.chiefdelphi.com/t/wcp-cadathon-2024-dunktank/472262)).
- Responsive, authoritative rules Q&A: the game designer answered interpretation questions in the thread within hours (e.g., the Hero Heist "district ownership" edge cases, story-panel material, extension-limit questions).
- Transparent judging and feedback: F4's live ranked feedback show and WCP 2024's individual written feedback were highly valued. The first question after the 2024 results was "Where can we find our individual feedback?"
- Public submissions: putting all CAD and documentation in a public thread creates a shared library of 100+ designs that teams learn from.
- Well-designed fictional games with real strategic depth (class systems, ownership mechanics) and fun themes.
- Timing: a November window (after offseason competitions, before build season) was credited with boosting participation compared with early October.
- Prizes with lasting value to a team (the TeamWCP sponsorship), plus rules that spread them to new teams.

### What frustrates participants
- Judging delays and silence: the 2025 results slipped a month past the scheduled date (landing 3 days before kickoff), and the People's Choice award was still unannounced 7+ months later. This produced persistent thread-watching and memes and was the most common complaint about the WCP event.
- Dropping individual feedback: WCP's 2025 note of "no individual feedback due to bandwidth" removed what participants said was a key reason to enter.
- Rules ambiguity without a formal Q&A system: Hero Heist's ownership-scoring wording led to debates over several interpretations (one reading allowed a 20–30-point exploit loop). With only in-thread answers, participants had to hope their question was seen. Andrew_L has said rules should be "taken as intended to minimize lawyering," but that consistent, transparent communication is what keeps ambiguity from souring an event.
- Opaque judging: the WRRF thread is the standard checklist of what erodes trust. It lists unnamed judges of unknown qualification, unstated criteria, no limits on manufacturing resources ("judges either too impressed by or too skeptical of 6-axis parts"), and no separation of all-student teams from mentor-heavy teams. As Andrew_L put it: "Anything left vague and ambiguous can sometimes (incorrectly) imply that things aren't figured out… The more information given up front, the more confidence people will have about your program."
- Fairness of team composition: stacked all-mentor or all-star cross-team squads competing against student teams are a recurring worry. F4 addressed it with size caps on mixed teams, and WCP with mentor ratios and honor-system requirements for student work; both approaches are imperfect but accepted.
- Scope and timeline: one week is regarded as roughly right for a full robot (F4's original 3 days was widely considered too short and was permanently extended). Conflicts such as travel and exams mean solo and small teams often submit work they know is unfinished, and teams whose training calendar did not line up could not participate well.
